Choice and payoffs

The agent chooses a level of unilateral control . At it acquires no additional control; at it achieves complete takeover. The model assumes the whole range is feasible, so the question is whether maximal control is worth choosing, not whether the agent could successfully attain it. Payoffs are measured in the agent's own utility units.

Control provides instrumental benefits, but obtaining and maintaining it can consume resources or disrupt valuable activity. We separate those effects into three functions:

We use quadratic benefit and cost functions as a tractable reduced-form approximation, not because control is known to have quadratic returns. Their linear marginal benefits and costs give a closed-form optimum and simple comparative statics. Quadratics can also approximate smooth functions locally to second order, though not necessarily across the whole interval. The central result does not depend on this functional form: gross benefits may increase throughout even when marginal costs and lost cooperation make further acquisition suboptimal.

Even if power has no diminishing gross benefit (), rising costs () can generate an interior optimum. And even if domination sacrifices no cooperation (), ordinary acquisition costs can do the same. Neither an aversion to power nor unusually benevolent objectives are required.

Conversely, if control is costless and destroys no independent surplus (), positive marginal instrumental benefit makes maximal control optimal. The model allows this outcome; it does not assume it away.

Higher initial instrumental value shifts the optimum toward more control. Higher initial cost or greater value from independent cooperation shifts it toward less. Faster diminishing gross benefits or faster rising costs also reduce the interior optimum. At the corner solutions, clipping holds the choice at zero or one until a threshold is crossed.

Scope of the result

The result is a logical separation, not an empirical forecast. The gross instrumental value of power can rise throughout the feasible range while the net-optimal level of realized control remains strictly below takeover. This is a one-agent, reduced-form decision problem; reactions by other actors enter only through its benefit and cost functions.

That probability cannot be evaluated without a defensible joint distribution over objectives and strategic environments. Nor does this simple concave specification describe thresholds, changing capabilities, or strategic feedback in full detail; with nonconcave payoffs, one must compare global payoff levels rather than rely on the slope at alone.
